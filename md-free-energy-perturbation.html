Part one

Equilibrium simulation and structural analysis

Approximately one microsecond of equilibrium MD per complex, across three structured FUS RNA-binding systems, analysed for RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, contact patterns, overall stability, and the effect of construct truncation. This built on earlier AMBER20 work on wild-type and P358L/P360S DPP III at 150 ns per system — the MSc-era study where the wet-dry loop was first closed end to end.

Protein–RNA systems are strongly charged and slow to equilibrate, so setup gets more attention than the production run: careful ion placement and counter-ion neutralisation, a force field chosen for nucleic-acid compatibility, staged restrained equilibration, and box dimensions large enough that a highly charged solute does not interact with its periodic image.

Post-processing is scripted end to end — trajectory centring and fitting to remove periodic-boundary jumps, then a standard battery of observables. The structural questions are answered at contact level: which residues touch the RNA, how often, and for how long.

What comes out

RMSD and radius of gyration for global stability, per-residue RMSF for local flexibility, Cα–C1′ distance maps for interface geometry, contact counts over time for interface stability, and residue-level contact-frequency heatmaps that make the binding interface directly comparable to the residue contributions derived from the peptide array.

Part two

A custom RNA nucleotide-transformation framework

Free-energy perturbation answers a question equilibrium MD cannot: how much does a specific substitution change binding? Most available tooling assumes amino-acid mutation. Transforming one nucleotide into another inside a bound RNA is a different problem — the perturbed group is larger, charged, stacked against its neighbours, and coupled to a backbone that will not hold still — and it needed a framework built for it, using GROMACS with SMArt-generated alchemical topologies.

The system is transformed along a coupling parameter λ from state A to state B in both the bound and free legs; the thermodynamic cycle gives the relative binding free energy.

ΔΔG_bind = ΔG_A→B(complex) - ΔG_A→B(free)

# BAR: solve self-consistently for ΔG between adjacent λ windows
Σ f(U_ij - ΔG) = Σ f(U_ji + ΔG),   f(x) = 1/(1 + exp(βx))

ΔG_total = Σ_λ ΔG_λ,λ+1

The practical work is in the details: soft-core potentials to avoid singularities as atoms appear and disappear, λ spacing dense enough that neighbouring windows overlap, advanced sampling where plain λ ladders were insufficient, and multiple independent trajectories rather than one long run. BAR and MBAR estimates are then checked for convergence — forward/backward hysteresis, phase-space overlap between adjacent windows, and block-averaged drift of the running estimate.

EDS topologies

Enveloping distribution sampling offers an alternative to a λ ladder: a single reference state that envelopes multiple end states, sampled in one simulation. Topology construction uses the SMArt library — an external, non-PyPI dependency by Dražen Petrov, referenced and attributed here, not redistributed.

Treating free energy as a sampling problem, not a tool run

An unconverged free energy looks exactly like a converged one if you only plot the final number. Every estimate therefore carries the running value against simulation time, the per-window contributions (a single dominant window is a warning sign), and replicate-to-replicate spread as the honest error bar — usually larger than the statistical error the estimator reports.

That discipline produced a negative result worth stating: the more dynamic protein–RNA complexes were not suitable for straightforward FEP. Hysteresis and sampling problems persisted through multiple trajectories and advanced sampling, and the conclusion drawn was that the method's assumptions did not hold for those systems — rather than reporting numbers the sampling did not support. Knowing where a free-energy workflow stops being valid is the part that transfers.
