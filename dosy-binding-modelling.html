Binding modelling in DOSY

The monomeric scale of the protein–RNA problem: do individual amino acids interact measurably with nucleotide monophosphates, and can that be quantified? Diffusion-ordered spectroscopy reports a population-weighted average diffusion coefficient, so in principle it can. In practice the interactions are weak, and most of the work is establishing what the measurement can and cannot resolve.

Contribution: I designed the experiments and models, prepared the samples, and analysed the results. NMR measurements and initial signal analysis were performed by specialist collaborators.

Principle

Diffusion as a binding observable

Each DOSY experiment is a gradient-strength series. Fitting the Stejskal–Tanner attenuation to the observed intensities gives a diffusion coefficient Dobs for every resolved resonance. Under fast exchange, that observable is the population-weighted mean of the free and complexed states — so a species that spends part of its time associated diffuses measurably more slowly than it would alone.

I(g) / I(0) = exp[ -D · γ²g²δ² (Δ - δ/3) ]

D_obs = (1 - θ)·D_free + θ·D_bound
        with θ = fraction associated

# with an inert internal standard (dioxane) to remove
# viscosity and temperature differences between samples
D_corr = D_obs · (D_ref,sample_0 / D_ref,sample_i)

The attraction of the method here is that it needs no label, no immobilisation and no separation step, and it works on species too small and too weakly interacting for most biophysical assays. The difficulty is the same fact from the other side: for amino acid–nucleotide pairs the effect on D is small, and it sits uncomfortably close to the reproducibility of the measurement itself.

Scope: the monomeric scale

This work sits at the smallest scale of a larger protein–RNA question. Rather than starting from a folded protein and a structured RNA, it asks whether the constituent chemistry — a single amino acid against a nucleotide monophosphate — already encodes measurable preference. Early tests on alanine and methionine were expanded into a broader monomeric framework across amino acids and NMPs.

Pipeline

  1. Experimental and model designSample series, concentration ranges, and the internal-standard scheme designed against what the method could plausibly resolve.
  2. Sample preparationMatched buffer, pH and ionic strength across the series, with dioxane as an inert internal diffusion reference.
  3. AcquisitionPerformed by NMR specialist collaborators, who also carried out the initial signal analysis.
  4. Per-point diffusion fittingStejskal–Tanner fits per resolved resonance, yielding D_obs with a standard error rather than a bare point estimate.
  5. Internal-standard correctionNormalise against the dioxane reference so that differences reflect association, not sample-to-sample viscosity and temperature drift.
  6. Modelling and sensitivity analysisFit association models to the corrected series — and, critically, simulate what interaction strength the concentration window and measurement precision could resolve at all.

What the method could and could not deliver

Interactions at this scale are weak and were treated as such. The analysis reports detectable but small effects together with an explicit statement of the sensitivity floor and the model assumptions — because the honest limit of the experiment is part of the result, not a caveat appended to it. In particular, this work does not support a universal amino-acid/nucleotide affinity scale; it constrains where such a description holds and where the measurement stops being informative.

Three failure modes are checked explicitly at the per-point stage: convection artefacts, gradient-calibration error, and non-monoexponential decay from overlapping resonances. Any of the three can manufacture an apparent interaction from a well-behaved sample.
